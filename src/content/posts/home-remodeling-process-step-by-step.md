A remodel feels chaotic from the kitchen table because you only see part of it: a dumpster arrives, walls disappear, then nothing seems to happen for a week, then everything happens at once. From the contractor's side it is a fixed sequence, and the quiet weeks are usually inspections and deliveries. Here is the whole sequence, what you are deciding at each step, and a real timeline from a house we took from bare studs to finished in four and a half months.

## Step 1: The call and the walkthrough

You call or send the form with a sentence about each room. We set a time to walk through the house, usually within a week. At the walkthrough we measure, open what we can (a basement ceiling tile, an attic hatch, the panel), and ask how you use the rooms. You should come away knowing roughly which cost tier you are in; our guides for [kitchens](/blog/kitchen-remodeling-trends/), [bathrooms](/blog/bathroom-remodeling/) and [basements](/blog/basement-finishing-pa/) are the same ranges we use.

**Your job:** have the one sentence per room ready, know your range, and tell us.

## Step 2: The written estimate

A few days after the walkthrough you get an itemized estimate: labor, materials, each trade, permit, allowances for things you will choose (tile, fixtures, cabinets). You compare it to others on scope, not on the total; our [guide to choosing a contractor](/blog/best-remodeling-company-pa-lancaster/) explains how.

**Your job:** read every line and ask about anything that is not there.

## Step 3: Contract, deposit and ordering

Pennsylvania requires a written contract for any home improvement job over $500: scope, price, payment schedule, start and completion dates, the contractor's registration number, and your three-business-day right to cancel. The deposit is capped at a third of the price plus special-order materials. Once it is signed, we order the long-lead items: cabinets (four to eight weeks), windows (two to six weeks), custom glass, special tile. Nothing is demolished until the dates for those deliveries are known.

**Your job:** make the material choices in this window. A kitchen waits on cabinet choices more often than on carpenters.

## Step 4: Permits

Structural, electrical and plumbing work needs a permit from your township or borough. We draw what the permit office needs, file it, and schedule the inspections into the plan. In most Lebanon County municipalities a permit takes one to three weeks. Trim, paint, flooring and cabinet swaps generally do not need one.

## Step 5: Protection and demolition

The first day on site is protection: dust barriers on doorways, floor protection along the path to the work, furniture covered or moved. Then demolition, to the studs if the plan calls for it. This is the week the house looks worst and the dumpster is full. It is also the week we find whatever the house was hiding, and we tell you the same day, with a price.

**Your job:** decide quickly on the surprise. The crew is standing in front of it.

## Step 6: Structure and rough-ins

Any framing changes come first: a wall out, a beam in, new stair stringers. Then the licensed electrician and plumber run the new lines while everything is open. Then the first inspection, which is the quiet week. Nothing is covered until the inspector has seen it.

## Step 7: Insulation, drywall and the second quiet stretch

Insulation goes in, drywall goes up, and then three coats of compound with drying time between each. Two days of hanging, then four or five days where it looks like nothing is happening. It is drying. Then primer, and the rooms suddenly look like rooms.

## Step 8: Floors, cabinets, tile

Floors go down before cabinets so they run under the toe kicks. Cabinets are set and leveled, then the counter is templated, which is another week's wait. Tile goes on walls and floors in the wet rooms, then cures before grout. Shower glass is measured after the tile and takes two to three weeks.

**Your job:** stay off the new tile for a day and off the new floors with heavy furniture until we say.

## Step 9: Fixtures, doors, trim

Plumbing fixtures, lights, outlets and switches, interior doors and all the trim. The final inspection happens here for electrical and plumbing.

## Step 10: Paint

Last, so nothing scratches it. Ceilings, walls, trim, doors. In a whole-house job this is two weeks on its own and it is the step that makes everything before it look finished.

## Step 11: The walkthrough and the list

We walk every room with you with a light and a notepad. Everything on the list gets fixed before the final payment. Then we come back if something needs attention a month later; a remodel is finished when you are still happy with it, not on the last day.

## A real timeline

In 2025 we took a [South Philadelphia rowhome](/projects/south-philadelphia-rowhome-renovation/) from bare studs to a finished house:

- **April:** demolition of the first floor and the old staircase.
- **May:** new stair stringers, replacement windows, drywall.
- **June:** vinyl plank floors, drywall finished and primed.
- **July:** staircase finished with oak treads and iron balusters, kitchen cabinets installed, cedar fence built.
- **August:** counters, backsplash, both bathrooms tiled and fitted, basement carpeted, front door and railings, final paint.

Four and a half months for a whole house. A single kitchen on its own is three to five weeks; a bathroom two to three; a basement three to eight.

## Living in the house while it happens

Most families stay home. What makes it workable:

- A sealed-off work zone and a clean path through the house.
- A temporary kitchen: microwave, coffee maker, a sink that works.
- One bathroom in service at all times, which we sequence for.
- Cleanup at the end of every day. Not a broad sweep at the end of the week.
- A daily word about what happens tomorrow.

What makes it fail: a crew that changes every day, decisions made at the last minute, and surprises nobody mentioned. We run the job to avoid all three.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does a remodel take?** A bathroom two to three weeks, a kitchen three to five, a basement three to eight, a whole house three to five months. Add the material lead time before the start.

**Why are there days when nobody is working?** Drying time for drywall and tile, and waiting for inspections and deliveries. A good schedule has them planned; a bad one has them as surprises.

**Can we change something halfway?** Yes, with a written change order and a price before the work. Changes after rough-in cost more than changes before.

**Do we need to move out?** For a full gut, usually yes. For one or two rooms, no.
